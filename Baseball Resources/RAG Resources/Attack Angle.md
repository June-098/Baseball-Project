## Attack Angle Explained

Attack angle is the angle of the barrel’s path immediately before contact.

### Positive attack angle

A positive attack angle means the barrel is traveling forward and slightly upward as it enters contact.

This should be a smooth, shallow rise—not a large uppercut. The hitter is still moving through the baseball toward the field.

A positive attack angle can help the hitter:

- Match the general plane of a descending pitch.
- Keep the barrel in the hitting zone longer.
- Create a larger opportunity for solid contact.
- Produce hard line drives and fly balls.
- Drive the ball from gap to gap.

### Negative attack angle

A negative attack angle means the barrel is traveling downward as it approaches the ball.

This is commonly created by instructions such as “swing down” or “chop down on the baseball.”

Because the pitch is generally descending, a sharply downward bat path crosses the pitch’s path instead of traveling with it. The bat and ball may share the same space for only a brief moment.

A negative attack angle can produce:

- Topped groundballs.
- Weak contact.
- Rollovers.
- Excessive backspin pop-ups if the hitter cuts under the ball.
- A small margin for timing errors.

## Attack Angle Versus Launch Angle

|Measurement|What it describes|When it occurs|
|---|---|---|
|Attack angle|Direction of the bat|Before and during contact|
|Launch angle|Direction of the batted ball|Immediately after contact|
|Exit velocity|Speed of the batted ball|After contact|

A hitter might have a positive attack angle but still produce different launch angles depending on where the barrel contacts the ball.

For example:

- Contact above the ball’s center can produce a groundball.
- Centered contact can produce a hard line drive.
- Contact slightly below center can create more lift.
- Contact too far below center can produce a pop-up.

Therefore, a positive attack angle is only part of the solution. The hitter must also square the baseball.


## The Wrong Approach: Chopping Down

A hitter who chops down sends the barrel on a steep, negative path toward the ball.

Imagine two roads crossing at an intersection. The pitch is traveling down one road while the bat quickly cuts across it. The bat can hit the ball, but the two paths meet for only a short distance.

If the hitter is slightly early or late, the barrel may miss the ball’s center.

This makes the swing less adjustable and often produces groundballs or inconsistent contact.

## Another Wrong Approach: Chasing Launch Angle

Some hitters hear “launch angle” and try to lift every pitch by:

- Dropping the back shoulder.
- Lowering the hands.
- Leaning backward.
- Scooping underneath the ball.
- Creating an exaggerated uppercut.

That is not the lesson in the video.

The goal is not to manufacture the highest possible launch angle. A very high launch angle can simply be a weak pop-up. The goal is to create a manageable positive attack angle, square the baseball, and produce useful batted-ball results.

## Step-by-Step Explanation

### 1. Begin in a balanced position

The hitter starts balanced and athletic, with the head stable and the hands connected near the rear shoulder.

The hitter should be able to see the pitch clearly and move without falling backward or lunging forward.

### 2. Load without dropping the barrel

As the pitcher begins the delivery, the hitter loads into the back hip and prepares the lower body.

The hitter should not drop the hands or barrel underneath the anticipated pitch. A positive attack angle should develop from the body’s rotation and the barrel’s turn—not from an artificial scoop.

### 3. Land in an adjustable position

The front foot lands while the hands remain back and the body stays balanced.

At this point, the hitter is prepared to recognize the pitch’s speed, location, and movement. The swing has not yet been forced upward or downward.

### 4. Turn the barrel onto the pitch’s plane

The hitter begins turning the barrel behind the ball.

Because most pitches are traveling slightly downward, the barrel should work forward and slightly upward into contact. This creates the positive attack angle discussed in the video.

A useful cue is:

> **“Forward first, with a slight rise.”**

The word “forward” prevents the hitter from exaggerating the uppercut.

### 5. Keep the barrel through the hitting zone

The barrel should continue through the area where contact can occur.

The hitter is trying to create a smooth path that stays compatible with the pitch’s path. This provides more room for small timing mistakes.

The goal is not merely to touch the ball. It is to keep the barrel in a position where it can square the ball over a longer portion of the hitting zone.

### 6. Square the baseball

At contact, the barrel should meet the ball solidly.

The point of contact on the ball helps determine its launch angle:

- Too high on the ball: groundball.
- Through the center: hard, lower trajectory.
- Slightly below center: line drive with lift.
- Too far below center: pop-up.

The best result combines solid contact, useful launch angle, and high exit velocity.

### 7. Allow launch angle to be the result

After contact, observe the ball’s trajectory.

Do not force the next swing higher simply because the previous result was a groundball. First determine why the groundball occurred. The hitter may have chopped down, contacted the top of the ball, or mistimed the pitch.

Launch angle is feedback about the result—not a command to lift every swing.

### 8. Evaluate the complete result

When technology is available, look at attack angle, launch angle, and exit velocity together.

A productive swing usually combines:

- A manageable positive attack angle.
- Centered contact.
- A useful positive launch angle.
- Strong exit velocity.
- Direction toward the middle or gaps.

The video does not provide specific target numbers, so the main standard is the quality and consistency of the contact.

## Example for Someone Who Has Never Seen the Video

Imagine a pitcher throwing a fastball that travels slightly downward toward home plate.

### Hitter A: Negative attack angle

Hitter A has been told to swing down. The barrel moves steeply from high to low and cuts across the pitch’s path.

If the timing is perfect, the hitter may make contact. But if the barrel catches the top of the ball, the result is a groundball. If it passes below the ball with a glancing blow, the result may be a weak pop-up.

The bat is moving across the pitch’s path for only a short time.

### Hitter B: Positive attack angle

Hitter B lands balanced and turns the barrel forward with a slight upward path.

The barrel follows the pitch’s general plane for a longer distance. When the hitter contacts the ball near its center, the ball leaves the bat as a hard line drive toward the gap.

Hitter B is not trying to lift the ball as high as possible. The hitter is creating a better path to square it.

That is the relationship:

> **The positive attack angle creates the opportunity; solid contact creates the productive launch angle.**

## How a Coach or Parent Can Check It

Record the hitter safely from the side and watch the swing in slow motion.

### Before contact

Look at the barrel’s direction immediately before it reaches the ball:

- Is it moving sharply downward?
- Is it moving forward with a slight rise?
- Is it scooping steeply upward?
- Does it stay in the hitting zone for more than one instant?

### At contact

Check whether the hitter remains balanced:

- Is the head stable?
- Is the chest collapsing backward?
- Did the hands drop to manufacture lift?
- Is the barrel square to the ball?

### After contact

Observe the result:

- Repeated groundballs may indicate a downward path or contact above the ball’s center.
- Repeated pop-ups may indicate an exaggerated uppercut or contact too far below center.
- Hard line drives suggest that attack angle and contact quality are working together.

One batted ball does not tell the whole story. Look for a repeated pattern over several swings.

## Goal of the Drill or Training

The goal is to develop a repeatable, slightly positive attack angle that allows the hitter to square the baseball and produce hard contact.

A successful hitter should be able to:

- Understand that attack angle belongs to the bat.
- Understand that launch angle belongs to the ball.
- Avoid chopping down with a negative attack angle.
- Avoid creating an exaggerated uppercut.
- Move the barrel forward with a slight upward path.
- Keep the barrel in the hitting zone longer.
- Square the baseball consistently.
- Produce hard line drives from gap to gap.
- Use launch angle and exit velocity as feedback.
- Make mechanical adjustments without chasing a single number.

The simplest coaching cue is:

> **“Create a slightly upward barrel path, square the ball, and let launch angle be the result.”**
