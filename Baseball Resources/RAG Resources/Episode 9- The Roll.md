## Create Separation for smooth roll and keep hitting tanks, homers, or good line-drives
## The Roll Drill: High-Level Summary

The Roll Drill teaches a hitter to create separation, keep the hands connected to the back shoulder, and move the barrel onto the pitch’s plane with a smooth “roll” instead of pushing the hands toward the ball.

The drill uses a basketball as a physical guide. Because the ball is large, the hitter can easily feel whether the hands and back elbow are staying in the correct position. It also makes an incorrect pushing movement much more noticeable.

Most importantly, “roll” does **not** mean rolling the wrists over. It means allowing the back elbow and barrel to work naturally into the hitting slot while the hands initially remain near the shoulder.

## Step-by-Step Explanation

### 1. Begin with the ball near the back shoulder

Hold the basketball near the back shoulder and hand area, with the back elbow elevated rather than collapsed against the body.

The ball creates a consistent starting position. It prevents the hitter from allowing the hands to wander or the back elbow to drop too early.

**What the hitter should feel:** The hands, back shoulder, and back elbow should feel connected as one organized unit.

### 2. Load the back side and shoulder blade

As the hitter begins the stride, the back shoulder blade—or scapula—loads slightly. The lower body starts moving forward, but the hands remain back near the shoulder.

This creates **separation**: the lower body gains ground while the upper body stays loaded.

Separation does not mean stretching the hands far behind the body. It means allowing the stride and lower body to move without carrying the hands forward too soon.

### 3. Land without committing the hands

When the front foot lands, the hands should still be close to the back shoulder and the back elbow should remain organized.

This is the drill’s most important checkpoint. Landing does not mean the hitter has already committed to swinging.

If the hands and elbow move forward before the foot lands, the hitter has very little time to recognize a breaking ball or changeup. If the hands remain back, the hitter can still swing, delay the swing, or stop completely.

### 4. Make the first move a roll

After the front foot lands and the hitter decides to swing, the first upper-body movement should be a roll of the back elbow into the slot.

The back elbow begins working down and around as the barrel turns behind the hitter. The hands should not immediately shove forward toward the pitcher.

Think of the back elbow beginning the movement while the hands remain connected near the shoulder for a fraction of a second.

### 5. Avoid pushing the ball forward

A push happens when the hands move directly toward the pitcher before the barrel has properly turned into the hitting zone.

This produces a steep, narrow bat path shaped like a **“V.”** The bat cuts down across the pitch’s path and then must quickly come back up. That leaves very little room for timing errors.

The correct roll creates a longer, smoother path resembling a **Nike swoosh**. The barrel enters the pitch’s plane earlier and stays through the hitting zone longer.

### 6. Use the basketball as feedback

The basketball helps reveal whether the hitter is rolling or pushing.

If the hitter cannot keep the ball organized near the shoulder during the load and initial turn, the hands or elbow are probably moving independently too early. The hitter should slow down and feel the back elbow initiate the move into the slot without throwing the hands forward.

### 7. Transfer the feeling to the bat

After several controlled repetitions with the basketball, repeat the movement with a bat:

1. Load the shoulder blade.
2. Stride and land with the hands still back.
3. Keep the back elbow organized.
4. Begin with the elbow-and-barrel roll.
5. Let the hands follow after the barrel starts turning.
6. Finish through a long, smooth hitting zone.

## Example: Adjusting to a Changeup

Imagine a hitter expecting a fastball. The pitcher throws a changeup instead.

A hitter who **pushes** begins moving the hands forward while the front foot is landing. By the time the hitter recognizes the slower pitch, the swing is already committed. The hitter may reach forward, roll over the ball, or miss completely.

A hitter trained with the Roll Drill lands with the hands still connected near the back shoulder. Because the hands have not fired, the hitter has extra time to recognize the changeup. The hitter can delay slightly, roll the elbow and barrel into the slot, and still deliver the barrel on plane.

That is the drill’s meaning of **adjustability**: the body is prepared to swing, but the hands are not committed prematurely.

## Goal of the Drill

The goal is to develop a repeatable launch position in which the hitter:

- Creates separation between the lower and upper body.
- Lands with the hands close to the back shoulder.
- Keeps the back elbow from collapsing prematurely.
- Preserves time to recognize and adjust to different pitches.
- Starts the swing with a roll instead of a hand push.
- Gets the barrel on plane early and keeps it through the hitting zone longer.

A successful repetition should feel **loaded, connected, adjustable, and smooth**—not rushed, steep, or forced.
