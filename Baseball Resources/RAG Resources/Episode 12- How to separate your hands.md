## Separation: High-Level Summary

Separation is the stretch created when the hitter’s lower body moves forward while the hands and upper body remain loaded near the rear shoulder.

Good separation does **not** come from actively pulling the hands backward. It comes from loading the rear shoulder blade—the scapula—while moving into the back hip and landing in a balanced position. This keeps the bat close to the body, improves barrel control, and gives the hitter more time to adjust to fastballs and off-speed pitches.

## The Wrong Way to Separate

The wrong method is often described as **“walking away from the hands.”** The hitter moves forward while deliberately leaving or pulling the hands far behind the body.

This may create a large-looking stretch, but it causes several problems:

- The bat moves too far from the hitter’s center of mass.
- The shaft loses its connection to the rear shoulder.
- The arms become disconnected from the torso.
- The barrel becomes harder to deliver consistently.
- The hitter may be late against fastballs.
- The hitter has difficulty adjusting to breaking balls and changeups.

More separation is not automatically better. If the hands travel too far away from the body, the hitter must pull the bat back into position before making an effective swing.

## The Right Way to Separate

Correct separation is created by loading the rear scapula while keeping the hands and bat close to the rear shoulder.

The lower body moves forward, but the hands do not need to make a large independent movement backward. From the pitcher’s perspective, it may look like the hands are moving away from the body. In reality, much of that space is created because the body is moving forward while the upper body remains loaded.

The key idea is:

> **Move forward with the lower body while keeping the hands connected to the rear shoulder.**

## Step-by-Step Explanation

### 1. Begin in an athletic stance

Start balanced, with the hands near the rear shoulder and the back elbow in a comfortable position.

The hitter should not begin with the hands excessively far away from the body. The bat should feel connected to the hitter’s center rather than supported only by the arms.

### 2. Begin the stride

As the front foot moves forward, the hitter begins gathering pressure into the back hip.

The lower body is gaining ground, but the hitter should not drag the hands forward with it. The hands remain organized near the rear shoulder.

### 3. Load the rear shoulder blade

During the stride, gently load the rear scapula by drawing the rear shoulder blade toward the spine.

This is a small, controlled movement—not an exaggerated squeeze or a forceful pull of the arms.

The scapular load allows the rear elbow to move behind the torso while the hands and bat remain connected to the shoulder area.

### 4. Keep the front side square

The hitter should remain relatively square to the plate during the load.

Do not create artificial separation by turning or “dumping” the front shoulder inward. Excessively closing the front shoulder can interfere with vision, balance, and pitch recognition.

The hitter should feel loaded into the back hip without allowing the upper body to twist excessively.

### 5. Land in a balanced 50-50 position

When the front foot lands, the hitter should be balanced and prepared to adjust.

At this checkpoint:

- The rear scapula is loaded.
- The rear elbow is elevated and visible behind the torso.
- The hands remain near the rear shoulder.
- The bat’s shaft remains close to the rear shoulder.
- The front shoulder has not collapsed inward.
- The hitter is balanced between the legs and ready to swing.

The hitter is now separated, but not yet fully committed.

### 6. Keep the rear elbow organized

The rear elbow should not collapse against the hitter’s side before the front foot lands.

An early elbow drop can cause the hands to disconnect, the barrel to flatten prematurely, or the hitter to push the bat toward the ball.

The elbow does not need to be lifted as high as possible. It only needs to remain organized behind the hitter so the barrel can enter the proper path.

### 7. Keep the bat near the rear shoulder

The bat’s shaft should remain in close proximity to the rear shoulder at landing.

The knob can generally point toward the catcher. The barrel may be slightly wrapped behind the head or held more vertically; that difference is largely personal style.

The critical point is the relationship between the bat and the hitter’s body. The bat should remain close enough to be controlled efficiently.

### 8. Swing from the connected position

Once the hitter recognizes the pitch and decides to swing, the barrel can turn from the loaded position.

Because the hands and bat stayed close to the body, the hitter does not have to recover from an excessively stretched position. The barrel can take a shorter, more direct route onto the pitch’s plane.

## How a Coach or Parent Can Check Separation

The easiest checkpoint is to stand in front of the hitter, on the pitcher’s side, and pause the movement when the front foot lands.

Look for the following:

1. **Can you see the rear elbow behind the hitter’s torso?**  
    If so, the rear scapula has likely loaded.
    
2. **Is the bat’s shaft still close to the rear shoulder?**  
    If it is far behind or away from the shoulder, the arms may be disconnected.
    
3. **Did the front shoulder remain controlled?**  
    The hitter should not create separation by turning the front shoulder excessively inward.
    
4. **Are the hands still near shoulder height?**  
    The hands should not drop below the shoulder or climb unnecessarily above it.
    
5. **Is the hitter balanced at landing?**  
    The hitter should be able to swing, delay, or stop without falling forward.
    

If the rear elbow cannot be seen from the front, the hitter may not have loaded the scapula correctly. If the elbow is visible but the hands and bat are extremely far from the body, the hitter may be over-separating with the arms.

## Example: Handling a Changeup

Imagine a right-handed hitter preparing for a fastball.

### Hitter using incorrect separation

The hitter strides forward and forcefully pulls the hands behind the body. At front-foot landing, the bat is far from the rear shoulder and the arms are stretched.

The pitch turns out to be a changeup. Because the bat is disconnected from the body, the hitter must bring it back toward the body before delivering it forward. The hitter loses time and barrel control, reaches for the ball, and produces a weak rollover.

### Hitter using correct separation

The hitter moves forward while loading the rear scapula. At landing, the rear elbow is visible behind the torso, but the hands and bat remain close to the rear shoulder.

When the hitter recognizes the changeup, the body can wait without the bat drifting farther away. The hitter then turns the connected barrel onto the pitch’s plane and drives the ball through the middle of the field.

That is why hitters such as Mike Trout and Mookie Betts are used as examples in the video: their compact separation helps them control the barrel and adjust to different pitch speeds.

## Goal of the Drill

The goal is to create a controlled, repeatable stretch between the lower and upper body without disconnecting the hands from the torso.

A successful hitter should be able to:

- Gain ground with the stride while keeping the hands back.
- Load the rear scapula instead of pulling the arms backward.
- Maintain pressure in the back hip.
- Keep the front shoulder controlled and square.
- Land with the rear elbow visible behind the torso.
- Keep the bat’s shaft close to the rear shoulder.
- Preserve the ability to swing, wait, or stop.
- Deliver a short, consistent barrel path against both fastballs and off-speed pitches.

A simple cue for the hitter is:

> **“Move forward, load the rear shoulder blade, and keep the bat close.”**
