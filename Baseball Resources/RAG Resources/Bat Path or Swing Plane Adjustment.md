## Getting on Plane with the Baseball: High-Level Summary

Getting on plane means turning the barrel so its path begins matching the incoming pitch’s path **before contact**, then keeping the barrel through that path for as long as possible.

The video argues that cues such as “be short to the ball,” “get on top of the ball,” and “take the knob directly to the ball” become harmful when hitters interpret them as pushing the hands forward and chopping the barrel downward.

The preferred sequence is:

> **Scap load, roll into the slot, create lag, and accelerate the barrel through the pitch.**

This gives the hitter a longer contact window, greater adjustability against off-speed pitches, and a better opportunity to drive the baseball.

## What Does “Getting on Plane” Mean?

A pitched baseball normally travels slightly downward as it approaches home plate.

The hitter’s barrel must enter that pitch path and travel with it through the hitting zone. The bat does not need to match the pitch perfectly, but the two paths should remain compatible for more than one instant.

Imagine two cars entering the same highway:

- A steep swing cuts across the highway and exits immediately.
- An on-plane swing merges onto the highway and travels along it.

The longer the barrel travels with the pitch, the larger the hitter’s opportunity for contact becomes.

## The Three Misleading Cues

According to the video, three common instructions can create a steep, inefficient swing when interpreted literally.

### 1. “Be short to the ball”

Being mechanically efficient is valuable, but some hitters interpret “short” as taking the hands and barrel directly to the ball.

That may produce the shortest physical distance, but it also creates a very small hitting window. The barrel approaches the ball steeply and passes through the pitch’s path for only a moment.

The better goal is a short, efficient movement **into the pitch’s plane**, followed by a long path through the hitting zone.

### 2. “Get on top of the ball”

A hitter may interpret this as turning the top palm down and chopping the barrel onto the baseball.

That creates a negative attack angle and can produce:

- Topped groundballs.
- Pull-side rollovers.
- A barrel that enters and exits the zone quickly.
- Difficulty covering pitches that move down or away.
- Reduced ability to drive the baseball.

The hitter’s eyes and posture should remain over the ball, but the barrel should not chop down across its path.

### 3. “Take the knob to the ball”

If the hitter drives the knob directly toward the baseball, the hands may move forward before the barrel turns into position.

This can leave the barrel behind the hands and force it to catch up late. The hitter may feel quick, but the barrel is not actually available in the hitting zone for very long.

The knob can remain organized on its track, but it should not be pushed directly at the baseball as an isolated hand movement.

## The Wrong Bat Path

The video demonstrates the wrong path by moving the top palm downward and taking the bat directly toward the ball.

This creates a narrow contact window:

1. The hands move first.
2. The top palm begins turning down.
3. The barrel becomes steep.
4. The bat cuts across the pitch’s path.
5. The barrel quickly leaves the hitting zone.

The swing may still make contact when timing is perfect. However, a small timing mistake can produce a miss or weak contact.

That becomes especially problematic against changeups and breaking balls because those pitches alter the hitter’s expected timing and location.

## The Correct G-Swing Sequence

The correct sequence has four major parts:

1. Establish a good scapular position.
2. Roll the forearm and barrel into the slot.
3. Create controlled barrel lag.
4. Accelerate the barrel forward through the hitting zone.

## Step-by-Step Explanation

### 1. Begin balanced and connected

Start in an athletic stance with the hands organized near the rear shoulder.

The hitter may use a personal hand position, but the hands should eventually become connected to the rear shoulder area before the swing begins.

The body should feel balanced enough to recognize and adjust to different pitches.

### 2. Load the rear scapula

As the hitter strides, load the rear shoulder blade slightly toward the spine.

This is the “good scap position” mentioned in the video.

At front-foot landing:

- The hands remain near the rear shoulder.
- The rear elbow is organized behind the body.
- The bat stays close to the hitter’s center.
- The front shoulder remains controlled.
- The hitter is balanced and adjustable.

The hitter should not create the scap load by forcefully pulling the hands away from the body.

### 3. Allow the body to begin turning

Once the hitter recognizes the pitch and decides to swing, the lower body and torso begin rotating.

The hands should not immediately race toward the ball. The body’s rotation creates the space and energy needed for the barrel to turn into the hitting slot.

### 4. Roll the top forearm

The top forearm helps turn the barrel behind the hitter and into the pitch’s plane.

This “roll” does **not** mean rolling the wrists over the baseball. It happens as the barrel is entering the slot, not as the hitter is prematurely turning the top palm down through contact.

The hitter should feel the rear elbow and top forearm organizing the barrel while the hands remain connected to the body.

### 5. Roll the barrel into the slot

The **slot** is the connected position from which the barrel can enter the hitting zone efficiently.

In the slot:

- The rear elbow is working near the hitter’s side.
- The hands remain connected to the rear shoulder and torso.
- The barrel begins turning behind the hands.
- The top palm has not hammered downward.
- The hitter remains balanced.

The hitter is now preparing the barrel to meet the pitch’s path rather than pushing the bat directly at the ball.

### 6. Create barrel lag

Lag means the barrel temporarily trails the hands and the body’s rotation while remaining ready to accelerate.

Lag should not be created by dragging the hands forward and leaving the barrel behind. It is a brief, natural relationship created by the body turning while the forearms organize the barrel.

In a good lag position:

- The body has started rotating.
- The hands remain connected.
- The barrel is turned into a powerful position.
- The hitter has not released the barrel prematurely.
- The barrel is prepared to accelerate onto the pitch’s plane.

Mike Trout is used in the video as an example of rolling the forearm into this lag position.

### 7. Enter the pitch plane early

“Early” does not mean beginning the entire swing too soon. It means placing the barrel onto the pitch’s path before the expected contact point.

This distinction is important:

- Starting the swing early can make the hitter vulnerable to off-speed pitches.
- Getting the **barrel on plane early** creates more adjustability.

The hitter wants the barrel available behind the ball while still controlling when contact occurs.

### 8. Accelerate the barrel forward

After the barrel enters the plane and reaches lag, it accelerates forward through the hitting zone.

The video calls this “snapping the barrel forward.” This should not be interpreted as an isolated wrist flick. The acceleration comes from the body’s rotation transferring energy through the connected arms and hands into the barrel.

The barrel should move forcefully through the field, not simply downward toward the baseball.

### 9. Remain through the hitting zone

Once the barrel enters the pitch’s plane, it should stay through the contact area.

This creates a larger hitting window. If the hitter is slightly early, the barrel may still contact the ball farther forward. If the hitter is slightly late, the barrel may still be available deeper in the zone.

The exact contact quality changes, but the hitter has more opportunities to put the barrel on the baseball.

## Why Getting on Plane Helps Against Off-Speed Pitches

A hitter usually begins timing the swing for a fastball. A changeup arrives later, while a breaking ball may also move down or away.

With a steep swing, the barrel crosses the pitch’s path for only a moment. If the pitch is slower than expected, the barrel may pass through the hitting zone before the ball arrives.

An on-plane barrel remains available longer. The hitter can delay the final acceleration and still have an opportunity to square the slower pitch.

Getting on plane does not eliminate the need to recognize pitches. It gives the hitter more room to adjust after recognition.

## Example: Fastball Timing Against a Changeup

Imagine a right-handed hitter expecting a 94-mph fastball. The pitcher throws an 82-mph changeup.

### Hitter using the “knob to the ball” approach

The hitter recognizes what appears to be a fastball and pushes the hands directly forward.

The barrel stays behind the hands before cutting steeply through the hitting zone. Because the changeup arrives later, the barrel passes through too early.

The hitter may:

- Swing over the ball.
- Reach forward with the hands.
- Roll the ball weakly to the pull side.
- Lose balance while trying to slow the swing.

### Hitter who gets on plane

The hitter lands in a good scapular position with the hands connected near the rear shoulder.

The body begins rotating, the top forearm rolls the barrel into the slot, and the barrel reaches an on-plane lag position. When the hitter recognizes the changeup, the final acceleration can wait slightly.

Because the barrel is traveling along the pitch’s path, it remains available long enough to meet the slower ball and drive it through the middle of the field.

## Example: Handling a Breaking Ball

Imagine a breaking ball that begins near the middle of the plate and moves down and away.

A hitter chopping down has a barrel path that is already crossing steeply through the zone. As the ball moves lower and farther away, the bat quickly loses coverage.

An on-plane hitter rolls the barrel into the slot and keeps it traveling through the pitch’s general path. This gives the hitter a better opportunity to cover the ball’s downward movement and drive it toward the opposite-field gap.

## Short to the Ball Versus Long Through the Zone

The video’s lesson does not mean the hitter should make a long, slow swing.

The ideal swing can be described as:

> **Efficient into the plane and long through the plane.**

That means:

- No unnecessary hand movement before the swing.
- No casting the barrel away from the body.
- No direct hand push toward the baseball.
- An early barrel turn into the pitch’s plane.
- A long path through the contact area.
- A powerful release through the field.

The swing is compact around the body but provides a long opportunity for contact.

## How a Coach or Parent Can Check the Swing

Record the hitter safely from the side and watch the swing in slow motion.

### At front-foot landing

Check:

- Are the hands near the rear shoulder?
- Is the rear scapula loaded?
- Is the rear elbow organized?
- Is the hitter balanced?
- Has the barrel already been pushed away from the body?

### During the first move

Check:

- Does the body begin turning before the hands push forward?
- Does the top forearm help roll the barrel into the slot?
- Is the top palm hammering down?
- Is the barrel becoming steep?

### Before contact

Check:

- Is the barrel beginning to match the pitch’s plane?
- Has the hitter created controlled lag?
- Is the barrel available behind the expected contact point?
- Are the hands still connected to the body?

### Through contact

Check:

- Does the barrel remain through the zone?
- Is the hitter driving the ball through the field?
- Does the bat immediately cut downward and exit?
- Is the hitter balanced enough to adjust?

## A Simple On-Plane Practice Progression

### 1. Slow-motion roll rehearsal

Without a ball, move slowly through the sequence:

1. Load the rear scapula.
2. Land balanced.
3. Begin rotating.
4. Roll the top forearm and barrel into the slot.
5. Pause in the lag position.
6. Continue the barrel through the hitting zone.

The hitter should feel the barrel turning behind the hands instead of the hands pushing directly toward an imaginary ball.

### 2. Three-position rehearsal

Pause at three checkpoints:

**Position 1: Landing**

- Hands connected to the rear shoulder.
- Scapula loaded.
- Body balanced.

**Position 2: Slot and lag**

- Rear elbow organized.
- Barrel turning into the plane.
- Hands have not disconnected.

**Position 3: Contact**

- Barrel square.
- Top palm generally up.
- Barrel traveling through the field.

### 3. Tee drill

Place the ball near the middle of the plate.

Take controlled swings and try to produce firm line drives through center field. Focus on rolling the barrel into the plane before accelerating through contact.

Avoid pushing the knob directly at the tee.

### 4. Two-tee path check

Place one tee with a ball at the contact point. Place a second empty tee slightly behind it and close to the intended incoming pitch path.

The barrel should travel above the rear tee and through the ball without chopping steeply or striking the rear tee. Adjust the equipment carefully for the hitter’s height and swing.

The objective is to create a smooth entry into the hitting zone, not an exaggerated upward swing.

### 5. Off-speed front toss

Once the movement is consistent, mix faster and slower front-toss pitches.

The hitter should use the same load and initial barrel move while adjusting the final timing. This tests whether getting on plane actually improves the hitter’s ability to handle speed changes.

## Goal of the Training

The goal is to create an efficient barrel path that gets onto the incoming pitch’s plane early and remains through the hitting zone.

A successful hitter should be able to:

- Load the rear scapula while staying connected.
- Avoid pushing the knob directly at the ball.
- Avoid turning the top palm down and chopping.
- Use the top forearm to roll the barrel into the slot.
- Create controlled barrel lag.
- Enter the pitch’s plane before contact.
- Accelerate the barrel without an isolated wrist flick.
- Remain through the hitting zone longer.
- Handle fastballs, changeups, and breaking balls.
- Produce fewer swings and misses.
- Drive the baseball with greater consistency.

The simplest coaching cue is:

> **“Don’t cut across the pitch—roll into its path and stay with it.”**
